When the news arrives heavy with death—from collapsing earth, from weapons, from spreading disease, and from the removal of refuge for those already displaced—the impulse is to demand explanation or to numb ourselves against it. Yet Lamentations offers a different posture: it names grief without requiring it to mean anything, and it holds together two truths that seem to contradict. The text does not say suffering is good, or that all will be well, or that those who grieve deserve it. It says instead that grief is real, that God does not delight in it, and that his mercies are great enough to coexist with our deepest anguish. On a day when the vulnerable bear the heaviest toll, perhaps the beginning of faith is simply to lament truly, and to trust that mercy is not withheld even when understanding is far away.
