On a day when the news carries the weight of conflict, loss, and displacement across continents, there is something in this ancient word that does not deny the reality of suffering but insists on a deeper truth: that mercy persists. The writer of Lamentations knew desolation intimately—the city burned, the people scattered—yet found each morning a new mercy, not because the crisis had passed, but because faithfulness endures even in ruin. When families stretch one meal across seven mouths, when thousands mourn recovered dead, when strangers show unexpected kindness despite rising hostility, when diplomats labor for peace—these are not signs that suffering has ended, but invitations to notice where mercy still moves. The question is not whether we deserve what we face, but whether we can see, in each new morning, the faithfulness that remains.
