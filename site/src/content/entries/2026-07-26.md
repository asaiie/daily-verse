The news today speaks of sudden violence, creeping disaster, and the slow erosion of safety and home. People flee fires not knowing what remains; families search satellite images for the houses they cannot reach; crowds gather in solidarity only to face attack. It is a day that asks not for explanation but for presence—for the willingness to sit with loss rather than rush past it. The ancient voice of Lamentations knew this weight: grief is real, and it does not resolve quickly. Yet even in that book written in exile, there is a whisper that sorrow does not have the final word. To read these verses is not to deny today's pain, but to ask whether, beneath the breaking, there might be room for mercy to work itself out over time—not as easy comfort, but as the slow, stubborn faithfulness of one who has not forgotten.
