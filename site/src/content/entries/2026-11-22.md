When we survey a world fractured by violence, betrayal of trust, environmental collapse, and the voicelessness of the vulnerable, the natural response is to despair. Yet the prophet's testimony—drawn from the ruins of a destroyed city—invites us to notice something else: that mercy itself persists, that it renews, that it is not dependent on our circumstances improving first. The word translated "faithfulness" carries the weight of reliability, of a constancy that shows up regardless of how the morning's news reads. This is not an argument that suffering is acceptable or that justice is unnecessary. Rather, it is an invitation to see that alongside the wreckage, there are always those who return to rebuild, who teach children to read, who tend to the displaced—small witnesses to a faithfulness that the news cycle cannot contain. What would it mean to trust that mercy itself is at work, even now, even here?
